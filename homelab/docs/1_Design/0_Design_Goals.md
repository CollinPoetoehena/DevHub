# Personal Goals

> **Why goals matter:** The first step of your home lab journey is to define clear goals. These goals will be your guiding principles, helping you define your design/architecture, determine what hardware to acquire, and prioritize your learning objectives. There is no one-size-fits-all approach — your goals should reflect your personal interests and learning priorities. In a homelab, you are free to pursue what excites you the most.

The personal goal of my home lab is simple: **learn the fundamentals of DevOps and Software Engineering, and have fun doing it — while building a cool and satisfying home lab to experiment with.** Topics include networking, Linux, Kubernetes, monitoring (Grafana, Prometheus), infrastructure automation, and more — because I genuinely enjoy experimenting with these things and want to advance my Engineering skills.

There's something genuinely cool and satisfying about having a real home lab cluster sitting on your desk: physical nodes, real networking, services you deployed yourself, dashboards showing live metrics from your own hardware. It's not just a learning environment — it's a playground to break lab workloads, try out new tools before using them at work, and experiment without making the household depend on those experiments, as explained in the [shared network design](3_Design_Network.md#chosen-one-shared-gateway-with-segmentation). And it just looks and feels awesome.

In my work I use these techniques daily and will continue to do so: VMs, networking, Linux, containers, etc. Having hands-on experience with them makes my work easier and more enjoyable, and makes me a better Engineer overall. A home lab is the best way to get that unrestricted, persistent environment where you can break things, fix them, and learn without consequences.

## What this is NOT

This lab is purely for learning and fun — not production, not an obligation.

Personally, I work as a DevOps Engineer, not as a hardware, network, or storage engineer. That means this lab is mostly about broadening my knowledge and following personal interest, but not about mastering hardware, networking, or storage topics at a deeply advanced specialist level like hardware, network, or storage engineers do. It is focused on theory, practical understanding, and some more advanced concepts, but not on the most advanced specialist work a dedicated network, storage, or hardware engineer would do.

**This is not a self-hosting / home data center project.** Some people enjoy turning their home lab into a full self-hosted platform — replacing cloud services like OneDrive, Netflix, Spotify, running their own VPN, NAS, DNS, mail server, and making their entire household depend on the lab. That's a perfectly valid hobby, but it's not the goal here. That kind of setup essentially becomes a second job: you need to maintain uptime, handle backups, deal with security patches, manage storage, and if the lab goes down, the whole house is affected — no media, no files, no internet services. It demands production-level reliability from what is supposed to be a learning environment.

This lab is the opposite for myself: it's for **learning, experimenting, and having fun**. Breaking a lab workload or rebuilding the cluster should not stop household services: the shared network foundation runs independently. That foundation does need maintenance, and gateway changes can affect the house, so it is kept stable rather than used as the experimental playground. The lab itself should remain something I enjoy tinkering with, not something I am obligated to keep running. Keep experiments separate from essential household dependencies; a shared physical network does not change that goal. See the [network's maintenance, recovery, and safe-experiment boundaries](3_Design_Network.md#maintenance-recovery-and-safe-experiments).

The networking boundaries below summarize the chosen setup. See [Network Design](3_Design_Network.md) for the topology and policy, and [Network Setup](../2_Setup/2_Setup_Network.md) for implementation documentation; its historical procedure is marked superseded until the current GUI setup steps are verified.

Key boundaries:

- **Not 24/7 uptime required**: can turn it off when done for the day or keep it running depending on preference, no stress.
- **Not self-hosting everything**: still using cloud storage (e.g. OneDrive) and streaming services (e.g. Netflix). Household internet must not depend on a lab-hosted DNS server, cluster, storage system, or management VM. A NAS or VPN can be considered deliberately later, but neither is required just to have a learning lab. See the [shared gateway's independence from lab services](3_Design_Network.md#chosen-one-shared-gateway-with-segmentation).
- **Maintain the home network, with as little overhead as possible**: I now own the home network's routing, firewall, switching, and Wi-Fi. That network needs maintaining anyway; putting a second lab router behind it would not remove that responsibility, only add another device and configuration lifecycle. The [network design](3_Design_Network.md) therefore uses one physical gateway and shared network hardware, with separate home, lab, management, IoT, and guest segments. This is one managed infrastructure, not one flat LAN.
- **Reliable foundation, disposable experiments**: the shared network stays independent of the lab compute platform, so shutting down or rebuilding the lab does not stop household internet, DHCP, DNS, or Wi-Fi. A shared gateway is still a shared failure domain: updates and firewall changes need care and a recovery plan. The aim is minimal maintenance, not no maintenance or a guarantee that network mistakes cannot affect the home. See [Maintenance, Recovery, and Safe Experiments](3_Design_Network.md#maintenance-recovery-and-safe-experiments).
- **Automation is a means, not an obligation**: use IaC where it saves work and supports learning. For the essential network, a small documented set of GUI changes, private configuration backups, and tested recovery is preferable to developing and maintaining custom automation. The chosen [hardware](1_Design_Hardware_Prerequisites.md#part-1--networking-hardware) and [software](2_Design_Software_Prerequisites.md#router--firewall-software) trade some flexibility for substantially less ongoing work. See the [network configuration and recovery approach](3_Design_Network.md#maintenance-recovery-and-safe-experiments).
- **Learn deeper networking in isolation**: experiment with router operating systems and advanced routing in isolated lab VMs rather than on the household gateway. Learning does not require rebuilding a router stack by hand or making the household depend on an experiment. See the [network's safe-experiment boundaries](3_Design_Network.md#maintenance-recovery-and-safe-experiments).
- **Not advanced hardware tinkering**: the focus is on DevOps concepts (K8s, automation, observability), not on assembling servers or deep hardware engineering.
- **Repurpose old hardware**: rather than buying new, old laptops and refurbished enterprise-grade mini PCs are used — good for the environment, good for the wallet, and still perfectly capable for learning.

The choices made in this home lab are based on these personal goals and interests.
